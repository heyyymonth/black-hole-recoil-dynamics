\title{Companion access after black-hole recoil: dynamical benchmarks and sparse binary populations}
\author{Venkata Hemanth Athota}
\email{bharadwaj.hemanth.002@gmail.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher}

\begin{abstract}
Retention after a black-hole merger is only one requirement for subsequent growth: a displaced remnant must encounter suitable companions. We investigate which reduced descriptions preserve that access through orbital benchmarks, a velocity-dependent passage calculation, a frozen cluster-potential comparison, and an audit of released late-time cluster populations. In a static Plummer potential, a first-return-then-central prescription misses early density exposure and eventually overestimates it. In a specified focusing-dominated binary bath, density exposure also overestimates normalized passage counts for the selected larger kicks. An evolved cluster snapshot shows that matching host mass and half-mass radius does not reproduce its ballistic return times. The broader population audit finds small instantaneous binary-black-hole counts throughout the inspected late-time states, extending the scarcity seen in the individual snapshot. These results motivate a falsifiable hypothesis: the temporal overlap of remnant motion with changing target availability contains predictive information beyond return time and a stationary population description. The hypothesis concerns environments conditional on remnant-producing mergers; equal-weight old-cluster states do not measure that population. Independent numerical checks support the reported benchmarks and census, while the coupled live dynamics, acquisition probabilities, and repeated-merger rates remain uncalibrated. The contribution is a tested set of approximation boundaries and a specific route to assessing companion access in hierarchical black-hole assembly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
A retained merger remnant need not remain in the dense part of its host. Another merger requires the remnant to reach suitable companions, acquire or enter a binary, and undergo subsequent evolution. Relating this sequence to gravitational-wave source populations requires knowing which environmental information survives a reduced dynamical description. We focus on one link: \emph{how accurately can remnant motion and an evolving binary population be reduced to a prediction of companion access?} Here access means a declared close passage to an existing binary black hole (BBH); other acquisition channels and the subsequent encounter outcome require separate treatment.

Published work already treats recoil displacement and frictional recentering~\cite{Islam2026}, host evolution and pairing~\cite{Torniamenti2024}, and successive mergers with explicit few-body encounters~\cite{Atallah2023}. Mass segregation and the coupling of black-hole subsystems to their hosts are established~\cite{Breen2013}. Small coexisting BBH populations and their formation, destruction, and ejection have also been reported~\cite{Downing2010}. The opportunity addressed here is to test the adequacy of reduced accessibility prescriptions against these physical ingredients, rather than to introduce them as new mechanisms.

We organize the investigation by the assumption being tested. A ballistic-return benchmark in a Plummer potential~\cite{Plummer1911} establishes the orbit; residence and density-exposure comparisons then test whether a central-return prescription preserves its environmental sampling. A specified tracer-binary bath isolates the effect of velocity weighting on close-passage counts. An evolved Cluster Monte Carlo (CMC) snapshot tests the assumed target population and potential shape~\cite{Rodriguez2022,RuiSnapshot}. Finally, an age-selected audit of available late-time summaries tests whether the snapshot's small BBH population is confined to that one state. Each comparison changes the information being supplied, rather than merely enlarging the same kick grid.

The results motivate the hypothesis that temporal overlap between remnant motion and changing target availability adds predictive information beyond a return time and stationary target description. Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis} defines the corresponding conditional quantity and a comparison that could reject the hypothesis. The present work supplies its motivation and approximation tests; it does not measure that overlap in live clusters. In particular, the passage calculation uses an imposed bath, the frozen-potential comparison uses a different host and physical kicks, and the population census is not conditioned on mergers. Their discrepancies cannot be combined into a calibrated rate correction.

Our ballistic center crossing is distinct from frictional recentering and does not reproduce or falsify the prescription of Ref.~\cite{Islam2026}. Within the fixed one-parameter Plummer family, an exact return time and the governing equations can identify the orbit; the error lies in replacing it by an assumed residence history. No observed gravitational-wave signals, radiation waveforms, or merger outcomes are analyzed. The long-term physical objective is to establish which assembly-history information an accessibility model can carry into predictions of repeated mergers; this paper tests the inputs needed for that step.

\section{Model and numerical method}\label{sec:model}
With host mass $M$, scale radius $b$, and gravitational constant $G$, define
\begin{align}
\Phi(r)&=-\frac{GM}{\sqrt{r^2+b^2}},\label{eq:potential}\\
t_d&=\sqrt{\frac{b^3}{GM}},\qquad
\eta=\frac{v_k}{\sqrt{2GM/b}}.\label{eq:units}
\end{align}
Let $x$ be signed displacement in units of $b$ and let time be measured in $t_d$. The initial-value problem is
\begin{align}
\ddot{x}&=-\frac{x}{(1+x^2)^{3/2}},\label{eq:motion}\\
x(0)&=0,\qquad \dot{x}(0)=\sqrt{2}\eta,\label{eq:initial}
\end{align}
with conserved dimensionless specific energy $E=\eta^2-1$.
We restrict attention to $0\leq\eta<1$. For a nonzero kick, $P$ is the interval between successive center crossings. Signed position has period $2P$; radius and density have period $P$. The exactly stationary case has no return event and is treated separately.

\subsection{Ballistic return benchmark}\label{sec:return-method}
Energy conservation yields the apocenter
\begin{equation}
x_a=\frac{\eta\sqrt{2-\eta^2}}{1-\eta^2}\label{eq:apocenter}
\end{equation}
and return time
\begin{equation}
P=2\int_0^{x_a}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2[(1+x^2)^{-1/2}-(1-\eta^2)]}}.\label{eq:period}
\end{equation}
Set $x=x_a\sin\theta$, $s=\sqrt{1+x_a^2\sin^2\theta}$, and $s_a=(1-\eta^2)^{-1}$. The endpoint singularity is removed:
\begin{align}
\frac{dt}{d\theta}&=\sqrt{\frac{s s_a(s+s_a)}{2}},\label{eq:transform}\\
P&=2\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{dt}{d\theta}\,d\theta.\label{eq:regular-period}
\end{align}

\subsection{Residence and density-exposure comparison}\label{sec:exposure-method}
For horizon $H$ and operational sphere radius $cb$, define
\begin{align}
C(H,c)&=\int_0^H\mathbf{1}_{|x(t)|<c}\,dt,\label{eq:residence}\\
I(H)&=\int_0^H(1+x(t)^2)^{-5/2}\,dt.\label{eq:exposure}
\end{align}
Here $C/H$ is a residence fraction, while $I/H$ is the mean host density relative to its central value. Neither is an encounter probability. The comparisons for nonzero kicks are
\begin{align}
I_{\mathrm{central}}(H)&=H,\label{eq:central}\\
I_{\mathrm{wait}}(H)&=\max(H-P,0);\label{eq:wait}
\end{align}
both equal $H$ for a stationary object.

The production calculation uses energy quadrature with absolute and relative tolerances of $10^{-11}$. Exposure integrals are split at radii 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 when these lie on the orbit. This resolves narrow central passages at large apocenter. Complete periods accumulate exactly; the partial period is recovered by a bracketed inversion of outgoing travel time. Core crossing times are found by quadrature, not by a time-sampled indicator.

\section{Velocity-weighted companion access}\label{sec:encounters}
\subsection{Specified population and comparison}
We set $M=10^6\,M_\odot$, $b=1\,\mathrm{pc}$, and remnant mass $m_{\mathrm r}=60\,M_\odot$. An imposed population of $N_{\mathrm b}=100$ tracer binary black holes has total mass $m_{\mathrm b}=60\,M_\odot$ per binary and semimajor axis $a=0.1\,\mathrm{AU}$. Its mass is $0.6\%$ of the fixed host mass. The unsegregated target number density is
\begin{equation}
n_{\mathrm b}(r)=n_0\widetilde\rho(x),\qquad
n_0=\frac{3N_{\mathrm b}}{4\pi b^3},\quad
\widetilde\rho(x)=(1+x^2)^{-5/2}.\label{eq:target-density}
\end{equation}
The target center-of-mass velocities follow the isotropic equilibrium Plummer distribution, not a measured black-hole subsystem.

All four cases, $\eta=0,0.3,0.8,0.9$, share $H=10$, corresponding to $T=Ht_d=0.149096\,\mathrm{Myr}$. They are admitted only if their apocenters lie within five Plummer half-mass radii, $6.52383b$. This operational screen is motivated by Ref.~\cite{Islam2026}; it does not truncate the potential or target distribution. The $\eta=0.99$ exposure example in Sec.~\ref{sec:exposure-results} fails that screen and remains an isolated-model limit. A common elapsed time is essential: the higher-kick cases have not completed one return by $H=10$, whereas the $\eta=0.3$ case has completed about 2.86 radial periods.

A close passage is defined by two-body monopole pericenter $R=1\,\mathrm{AU}$ from the target binary center of mass. This is ten binary semimajor axes, a boundary around the binary's neighborhood rather than a cross section for exchange. For relative speed $w$ before mutual focusing,
\begin{equation}
\Sigma(w)=\pi R^2\left[1+\frac{2G(m_{\mathrm r}+m_{\mathrm b})}{Rw^2}\right].\label{eq:cross-section}
\end{equation}
The local two-body approximation neglects host tides during the approach and internal binary multipoles. It does not determine the outcome after entry.

\subsection{Velocity weighting and the density approximation}
Let $u$ be target speed and $v$ remnant speed in the host frame. At fixed radius, $y=u/v_{\mathrm{esc}}(r)$ has density
\begin{equation}
p(y)=\frac{512}{7\pi}y^2(1-y^2)^{7/2},\qquad 0\leq y\leq1.\label{eq:speed-density}
\end{equation}
This follows from the isotropic Plummer distribution $f(E)\propto(-E)^{7/2}$~\cite{GalpyPlummer}. Isotropic angular averaging gives
\begin{align}
\langle w\rangle_\Omega&=\max(v,u)+\frac{\min(v,u)^2}{3\max(v,u)},\notag\\
\langle w^{-1}\rangle_\Omega&=\frac{1}{\max(v,u)}.\label{eq:angle-moments}
\end{align}
The velocity-integrated zero-speed limit is finite. We evaluate the remaining speed moments with incomplete beta functions and check them by direct numerical angle and speed integration.

Define the local encounter kernel and passage rate by
\begin{align}
K(r,v)&=\pi R^2\left[\langle w\rangle+
\frac{2G(m_{\mathrm r}+m_{\mathrm b})}{R}\langle w^{-1}\rangle\right],\notag\\
\Gamma(r,v)&=n_{\mathrm b}(r)K(r,v),\notag\\
\tau(T)&=\int_0^T\Gamma[r(t_{\mathrm{phys}}),v(t_{\mathrm{phys}})]\,dt_{\mathrm{phys}}.\label{eq:rate}
\end{align}
Here $K$ has dimensions of volume per time, $\Gamma$ inverse time, and $\tau$ is a dimensionless expected passage count along the unperturbed orbit. We do not turn $\tau$ into a merger probability or assume that the orbit remains unchanged after an actual encounter.

For implementation, use $V=\sqrt{GM/b}$ and dimensionless $t=t_{\mathrm{phys}}/t_d$. The geometric and focusing integrals are
\begin{align}
J_g&=\int_0^H\widetilde\rho\langle w/V\rangle\,dt,\qquad
J_f=\int_0^H\widetilde\rho\langle V/w\rangle\,dt,\notag\\
\tau&=n_0\pi R^2b\left(J_g+\lambda J_f\right),\qquad
\lambda=\frac{2(m_{\mathrm r}+m_{\mathrm b})b}{MR}.\label{eq:dimensionless-rate}
\end{align}
The length factor $b=Vt_d$ applies the time conversion once. In this configuration $\lambda=49.5036$.

The stationary central control defines $Q=\tau(\eta,H)/\tau(0,H)$. The density proxy is $D=I(H)/H$. Their relationship is exact for the specified bath:
\begin{equation}
\frac{Q}{D}=\frac{\int_0^H\widetilde\rho[x(t)]K[r(t),v(t)]\,dt}
{K(0,0)\int_0^H\widetilde\rho[x(t)]\,dt}.\label{eq:kernel-ratio}
\end{equation}
Thus the density approximation discards variation in the encounter kernel. It is accurate when the density-weighted mean of $K$ is close to its stationary central value; residence alone does not enforce that condition.

At $\eta=0.9$, density exposure is $5.81\%$ of the control but passage count is $2.93\%$. The density-only approximation is nearly twice the conditional velocity-weighted count. The geometric and focusing terms respond differently to motion. At $\eta=0.9$, their separately normalized contributions are 0.1110 and 0.02867, respectively, compared with $D=0.05811$. Higher relative speed increases geometric flux but reduces the inverse-speed contribution of focusing. Focusing supplies $97.23$--$99.19\%$ of the nonzero-kick counts and controls the total discrepancy. Slow motion near apocenter does not compensate for the low tracer density there.

Absolute counts remain small: the stationary control has $\tau=1.6080\times10^{-6}$ and the $\eta=0.9$ case has $\tau=4.7064\times10^{-8}$. These are conditional outputs for the imposed population and window, not an encounter-rate prediction for a calibrated cluster. A large fractional proxy error here does not establish a large merger-rate effect. The short window also does not prove that friction, diffusion, or host evolution would be negligible in a real system.

\section{Cluster population and potential comparison}\label{sec:snapshot}
\subsection{Reference state and comparison design}
We use the full evolved example distributed with \texttt{cmctoolkit}~\cite{Rui2021}: model \texttt{N8-RV1-RG8-Z0.1}, snapshot \texttt{initial.snap0382.dat.gz}, at the pinned source revision identified in Ref.~\cite{RuiSnapshot}. It was selected as an available evolved example before examining discrepancies. It is one simulated state, not an observational dataset or the full CMC catalog~\cite{Kremer2020}.

The age is $12.7323\,\mathrm{Gyr}$, total system mass $M=224633\,M_\odot$, and three-dimensional half-mass radius $r_h=4.79771\,\mathrm{pc}$. Matching these last two quantities gives Plummer scale $b=r_h\sqrt{2^{2/3}-1}=3.67706\,\mathrm{pc}$. This comparison uses current properties, not initial conditions or the imposed bath's host parameters. The numerical kick fractions recur across the studies, but their physical speeds are not the same across hosts.

We separately count all systems, all binaries, single black holes, binaries containing at least one black hole, and binaries containing two black holes (BBHs). Stellar-type code 14 identifies black holes in this input convention. The declared minimum count of 30 screens whether continuous BBH-distribution calibration is supported; it is not a statistical sufficiency theorem. Descriptive radial and speed CDF distances carry no independent-sample significance interpretation.

\subsection{Frozen spherical potential and identical kicks}
Each system is spread over a spherical shell at its snapshot radius. With zero potential at infinity,
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\mathrm{sh}}(r)=-G\left[\frac{M(<r)}{r}+
\sum_{r_i\geq r}\frac{m_i}{r_i}\right].\label{eq:shell-potential}
\end{equation}
The central value uses the finite limiting shell sum. Exterior mass contributes to potential energy even where its force vanishes. For central launch speed $v_k$, the energy is $\mathcal E=v_k^2/2+\Phi_{\mathrm{sh}}(0)$; the apocenter solves $\Phi_{\mathrm{sh}}(r_a)=\mathcal E$, and
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{sh}}=2\int_0^{r_a}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2[\mathcal E-\Phi_{\mathrm{sh}}(r)]}}.\label{eq:shell-return}
\end{equation}
We split the integral at shell boundaries and regularize the turning point. This is a frozen monopole calculation with no drag or encounters.

The snapshot and matched Plummer central escape speeds are $26.547$ and $22.924\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. We apply identical physical kicks, chosen as $0.3$, $0.8$, and $0.9$ times the latter speed. Renormalizing by each model's own escape speed would change the physical experiment. All three kicks are bound in both isolated potentials. This binding classification is distinct from tidal removal in CMC~\cite{Rodriguez2022}.

\section{Late-time availability of binary targets}\label{sec:catalog}
\subsection{Objective and sample definition}
The full snapshot leaves a population question that a denser kick grid cannot answer: is its small BBH target count confined to one saved state? We audit every standard-model summary in the same pinned \texttt{cmctoolkit} release~\cite{Rui2021,RuiSnapshot}. Selection rules were committed before reading the additional count outcomes. The input inventory contains 7537 summaries across 126 model histories; six separately tuned NGC~6624 models are excluded. The published CMC catalog contains 148 simulations~\cite{Kremer2020}, but not all appear in this late-time summary release. Unavailable histories are not assigned zero populations, and their absence does not establish disruption.

The primary comparison selects one saved state per model: the state closest to $12\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ within an inclusive $0.25\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ window, resolving ties by earlier age and then source path. This avoids weighting models by their output cadence. Seven histories have no eligible state; the 119 selections span $11.8013$--$12.2086\,\mathrm{Gyr}$. Equal model weights define a descriptive old-cluster comparison, not a probability distribution for observed clusters or merger epochs. Integer populations are not interpolated between saved states.

The target count is the number of binaries whose two stellar types are both 14. Total black-hole objects, BH--non-BH binaries, and all binaries remain separate populations. We check type-count sums, binary-pair accounting, the identity between system and object counts, and nonnegative single-BH membership. Every source path and age is retained. Model-level minima, medians, and maxima describe available saved states, not time averages or counts of distinct objects. Without binary identities, repeated appearances cannot be treated as new companions.

The selected states contain between zero and nine BBHs, with median two (Table~\ref{tab:catalog}, Fig.~\ref{fig:catalog}). There are 38 measured zero states and 71 states with at most two BBHs. By contrast, total BH-object counts range from zero to 1878 with median 38, and all-binary counts range from two to 142232 with median 25867. Neither larger population substitutes for the specified BBH targets. All 119 selected states fall below the inherited count-30 screen.

The complete set of saved summaries spans $10.0007$--$14.0022\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ and has maximum BBH count 15; no inspected state reaches the screen. These are simulation output ages, not a cosmological-age selection. The model used in Sec.~\ref{sec:snapshot} has 26 saved states with one to three BBHs and a saved-state median of two. Its near-$12\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ state contains three BBHs at $11.9449\,\mathrm{Gyr}$; its original full snapshot contains two at $12.7323\,\mathrm{Gyr}$. The small count therefore extends beyond the initially inspected state and throughout the available late-time release. This does not establish universal sparsity at other ages or in nuclear clusters.

The summaries provide aggregate counts but not the joint positions, velocities, binary masses, semimajor axes, eccentricities, or stable identities required to calibrate the passage calculation. None passes the operational single-state count screen; more fundamentally, abundance alone cannot supply those missing correlations. Sparse individual states may still admit a useful smooth ensemble description. A measured zero means no instantaneous target of this class, not that future formation or other acquisition channels are absent. Earlier cluster calculations already found small numbers of coexisting BBHs and rapid population turnover~\cite{Downing2010}; the present contribution is the reproducible scope and accounting of this pinned release. This census itself does not determine target lifetimes, turnover, or the probability of encounter during a remnant's return.

\section{Analytical and numerical verification}\label{sec:validation}
For a stationary object, $I(H)=C(H,c)=H$. For nonzero bound kicks, both exposure and occupancy repeat every $P$, hence $I(nP)=nI(P)$. In the small-kick limit, $I(P)\to\pi$ and $I(P)/P\to1$.

Near escape, the marginally bound outgoing trajectory obeys $\dot{x}=\sqrt{2}(1+x^2)^{-1/4}$. The limiting exposure of a full bound excursion is
\begin{align}
\lim_{\eta\to1^-}I(P)
&=\sqrt{2}\int_0^\infty(1+x^2)^{-9/4}dx\notag\\
&=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(7/4)}{\Gamma(9/4)}\notag\\
&\simeq1.0166557018.\label{eq:exposure-limit}
\end{align}
Combining this with Eq.~\eqref{eq:period-limit} gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{I(P)}{P}\sim\frac{\Gamma(7/4)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(9/4)}(1-\eta^2)^{3/2}.\label{eq:mean-limit}
\end{equation}
This is a limit from bound orbits. An exactly marginally bound particle does not return in finite time.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
\subsection{Claims supported by the combined evidence}
The orbital benchmarks show that a first-return-then-central prescription does not preserve exposure: it suppresses early sampling and eventually overestimates late sampling. The near-escape return time diverges while exposure per bound excursion remains finite. These are properties of the collisionless benchmark, not frictional settling estimates.

The passage calculation identifies another omission. Equation~\eqref{eq:kernel-ratio} shows that density weighting discards changes in the relative-velocity kernel. The selected larger kicks fail the operational approximation screen even when the orbit is accurately computed. This establishes a conditional approximation error, not a universal direction or magnitude of astrophysical bias.

The cluster comparisons constrain inputs that numerical accuracy alone cannot justify. Matching host mass and half-mass radius fails to preserve the selected snapshot's ballistic returns. Its two BBH targets cannot calibrate the imposed bath. The late-time census broadens the small-count evidence to the inspected release, without identifying the joint companion distribution. Changing the prescribed number of targets to the census median would leave velocities, concentration, binary properties, and merger conditioning unresolved. Likewise, multiplying the passage errors by the frozen-potential return ratios is unjustified: the comparisons use different hosts and do not calculate their coupled dynamics.

Together, the results identify three distinct requirements for a reduced accessibility model: adequate remnant motion, velocity-dependent passage weighting, and a target population appropriate to the remnant's environment and epoch. This is the supported synthesis. It does not establish a measured reacquisition rate, an observational formation-channel discriminator, or a new mechanism for hierarchical mergers.

\subsection{A hypothesis for merger-conditioned accessibility}\label{sec:hypothesis}
The next theoretical question is whether those ingredients can be reduced independently. Let $\mathcal M$ specify a preceding merger, its host state and remnant properties, conditional on retention. Let $N_{\mathrm b}(t)$ be the number of available BBH targets at elapsed time $t$, excluding the consumed progenitor binary and any system containing the tagged remnant. For a specified local passage model, define
\begin{align}
p_n(t\mid\mathcal M)&=\Pr[N_{\mathrm b}(t)=n\mid\mathcal M],\notag\\
g_n(t\mid\mathcal M)&=\mathbb E[\Gamma(t)\mid N_{\mathrm b}(t)=n,\mathcal M].\label{eq:conditional-rate}
\end{align}
The ensemble mean predicted passage count over a common physical window $T$ is then
\begin{equation}
\overline\tau(T\mid\mathcal M)=\int_0^T\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
p_n(t\mid\mathcal M)g_n(t\mid\mathcal M)\,dt.\label{eq:conditional-access}
\end{equation}
The rate $g_n$ incorporates target phase space and binary properties together with the remnant path. Equation~\eqref{eq:conditional-access} is a conditional-expectation identity, not an additional simulation result or an assumption of Poisson encounters. Zero targets imply zero instantaneous passage rate for this channel, but subsequent formation can change the population. A mean count alone does not determine the probability of at least one encounter.

Our working hypothesis is that, at fixed host and remnant properties, the temporal overlap of remnant motion with changing target availability provides predictive information about finite-window passage counts beyond a stationary target description and first-return time. The physical motivation is that a returning remnant samples companions present along its path during the relevant interval; an age-averaged population need not preserve that overlap. The present calculations establish neither its sign nor its size. In particular, the census measures equal-weight late-time states, not $p_n(t\mid\mathcal M)$, and no live trajectory is supplied for $g_n$.

This is a proposed test of a reduced description, not a claim that coupled population dynamics are absent from existing cluster models~\cite{Downing2010,Rodriguez2022,Kremer2020}. An earlier assembly model already permits new hard binaries to form while a retained remnant is inactive during recentering~\cite{Antonini2019}. Successive-merger and evolving-cluster frameworks also incorporate encounter and population physics~\cite{Atallah2023,Torniamenti2024}. The intended advance is a quantified domain of validity for reducing that physics to remnant accessibility. That advance would require validation beyond the present benchmarks.

\subsection{A comparison that could reject the hypothesis}
The required reference sample consists of merger-linked cluster histories with stable binary identities and adequately resolved target formation, exchange, destruction and ejection. Full snapshots must retain joint center-of-mass position and velocity, component masses, semimajor axis and eccentricity. Both younger and older epochs may contribute; selection at a fixed late age cannot substitute for conditioning on a remnant-producing merger. Parent binaries and consumed objects must be excluded from the subsequent target inventory, and repeated appearances must retain their identities.

First isolate population modeling: supply the same independently resolved remnant paths and the same close-passage definition to a stationary-bath model and a model retaining target-count evolution and joint properties. Match the host, remnant properties, observation window, and marginal target abundance, so a difference cannot be attributed solely to changing the normalization. Hold out entire cluster histories when assessing predicted counts against recorded encounters. Count-only corrections and corrections to joint phase space should also be assessed separately. Improvement must exceed numerical and ensemble uncertainty and persist on held-out histories, rather than arise only from additional fitting freedom.

The hypothesis would be disfavored in a declared regime if the stationary description already predicts passage counts within the required accuracy and a time-dependent description yields no stable predictive improvement, with sufficient precision to resolve a predeclared effect size. Conversely, a reproducible residual tied to target availability during displacement, removed by the extended model, would support retaining that information. Accuracy requirements, comparison statistics and sample selection must be fixed before those runs. The very small imposed-bath counts illustrate why a few zero-encounter paths would be uninformative; adequate ensemble size and a justified count model are essential. No such held-out comparison has been executed here.

Then isolate the trajectory approximation with the population treatment held fixed. Live collisional integrations, paired kicked and un-kicked runs, independent microstates, and varied kick directions can compare frozen-potential and central-residence prescriptions with inward core crossing after exit, residence, and recorded passages. Escape must be separated from finite-window non-return. Integration, close-encounter regularization, and changes to relaxation under particle-number scaling require independent checks. Phase-resampled CMC radii cannot supply continuous recoil paths~\cite{Rodriguez2022}.

The broader objective is a defensible connection between assembly environments and repeated-merger source populations. The present result is an evidence-based narrowing of that problem to companion accessibility. Even a successful accessibility model would still require exchange or capture, binary evolution, and merger outcomes before supporting a gravitational-wave population prediction.
